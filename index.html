01

PRMs vs ORMs — What Each Rewards

Both PRMs and ORMs are classifiers trained to evaluate model-generated reasoning. The distinction is granularity: where in the reasoning chain the reward signal is applied.

Outcome Reward Model (ORM)

Given a complete solution, predict correct / incorrect. Training signal: final-answer accuracy (symbolic check). Fast to label — thousands of examples per GPU-hour. Sparse signal: no feedback on intermediate steps, so the model can reach a correct answer via wrong reasoning.

Process Reward Model (PRM)

Given a solution prefix up to step k, predict whether this step is correct. Dense signal: every reasoning step gets a score. Harder to label — requires either human raters per step or automated step-level monte-carlo estimation. Trains more reliable reasoning behaviours.

Why the distinction matters for RL

With an ORM, a policy gradient update propagates reward from the final answer back through the full trace uniformly — every step gets credit whether it helped or not. With a PRM you can identify exactly which steps were erroneous and assign negative reward there, producing much sharper policy gradients. The downside: PRM training data is expensive.

PRM Construction — Step-Level Human Labels

OpenAI's PRM800K dataset (Lightman et al., 2023) is the canonical reference for human-labelled process rewards. Annotators were shown model-generated solution prefixes one step at a time and asked to rate each step: positive (correct, likely to lead to a correct answer), negative (incorrect), or neutral (correct but not helpful).

What counts as a step?

PRM800K uses a “natural step” segmentation — each paragraph of a solution is one step. The model itself generates the segmentation as part of its output format (a newline-separated chain-of-thought). Steps are typically 1–4 sentences of maths prose plus an equation.

03

Math-Shepherd & PRM800K

Two landmark datasets drove the field's understanding of process-level supervision. Their differences reveal the fundamental labelling trade-off.

PRM800K — Lightman et al., OpenAI 2023

800k step-level labels across 75k MATH solutions. Human annotators rated each step positive/neutral/negative. The resulting PRM, when used as a best-of-N selector, raised MATH accuracy by 20+ pp over ORM-selected best-of-N at N=1860. The PRM was released publicly; the model weights were not. Three-label scheme.

Math-Shepherd — Wang et al., 2023

Automated step-level labels via Monte Carlo estimation. For each prefix, K completions are sampled; fraction that reach a correct answer = estimated step correctness. Scales to arbitrary dataset size at inference cost only. Labels are noisier than human; performance lags PRM800K by ≈3 pp but costs 100× less to produce.

The MC estimation formula (Math-Shepherd)

For step sk in a partial solution, sample K completions from the policy conditioned on the prefix up to sk. Score each completion with a symbolic checker. PRM label = E[correct | prefixk] = count(correct) / K. Requires K×(max steps) forward passes per training example — typically K=8, 5–15 steps → 40–120 model calls per label.

04

MCTS-Guided Decoding

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) replaces greedy or beam decoding with a principled search over the reasoning tree. At each decision point, the tree is expanded by sampling candidate next-steps; the PRM scores each node; UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) selects which branch to explore next.

UCB selection formula

Upper Confidence Bound for Trees

UCB score for node n: Q(n) + c · √(ln N(parent) / N(n)) where Q(n) is the PRM value estimate (backed up from simulations), N is visit count, and c is the exploration constant (typically 1.0–2.0). The PRM replaces the rollout value function; no full simulation to terminal state is needed.

Verifier-Scored Sampling

Verifier-scored sampling is a simpler alternative to MCTS: generate N complete solutions independently from the policy, score each with a verifier (ORM or PRM), and return the highest-scoring one. No tree; no incremental expansion. The key design choices are: what to generate, how many, and what to score with.

Variants

Best-of-N (ORM)

Score final answers with an ORM classifier. Cheapest to implement. Effective when the ORM is well-calibrated. Diminishing returns after N≈64.

Best-of-N (PRM)

Score by minimum PRM score along the trace (“weakest-link”) or product of step scores. Better at catching flawed-reasoning paths that happen to reach the right answer.

Weighted majority vote

Weight each candidate's vote by its verifier score. Combines coverage (many answers) with discrimination (trust high-confidence ones). Effective for discrete answer spaces.

Scaling law for best-of-N

Empirically, MATH accuracy with best-of-N roughly follows accuracy(N) ≈ 1 − (1 − p)N where p is the per-sample probability of a correct answer. This saturates quickly for high-p tasks. For low-p tasks (Olympiad-level) even N=256 may be insufficient, and MCTS with a strong PRM outperforms best-of-N for the same compute budget.

Tree-of-Thought in Production

Tree-of-Thought (ToT; Yao et al., 2023) is a prompting-level framework that exposes tree-structured reasoning to the LLM as a zero-shot behaviour, without modifying the model or training a PRM. The model is asked to generate candidate intermediate thoughts, self-evaluate them, and continue from the most promising.

Imagine three independent experts are solving this problem step by step.
Each expert writes one step of their reasoning, then all steps are shared.
Any expert who realises their step is flawed silently drops out.
Continue until the answer is reached or only one expert remains.

Why ToT underperforms trained PRMs in practice

LLM self-evaluation of reasoning steps is poorly calibrated — the model tends to give high scores to confident-sounding but incorrect steps. A trained PRM with even 50k examples significantly outperforms ToT self-scoring. The original ToT paper used GPT-4 on Game-of-24 and crossword puzzles — tasks where self-evaluation is easier. On formal maths, ToT without an external scorer is near-random pruning.

07

Self-Consistency vs Verifier-Based

Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) is the cheapest ensemble approach: sample N answers and take a majority vote. No verifier needed. It works because independent samples are unlikely to make the same error, so the correct answer has higher multiplicity. The question is: when does a trained verifier beat majority vote?

When self-consistency fails

On tasks where the wrong answer is systematically more likely (the model has a consistent bias), majority vote amplifies the bias. A trained verifier is bias-agnostic — it scores the path, not the answer string. At small N (≤4), PRM best-of-N consistently beats majority vote by 5–10 pp in MATH experiments.

Cost-Quality Curves

Every inference-time search method has a cost-quality curve. The question an engineer must answer is: for a given accuracy target, which method is cheapest? The answer depends on base model quality, task type, and whether a PRM is available.

Snell et al. (2024) key finding

For easy–medium difficulty problems (MATH levels 1–3), best-of-N with an ORM is most compute-efficient. For hard problems (level 4–5, AIME), MCTS + PRM or a trained reasoning model dominates. The crossover point is where greedy-decode accuracy drops below ≈50%: best-of-N saturates, but sequential refinement (MCTS or native reasoning) still scales.

Practical engineering note

If you have no PRM and cannot afford to train one, self-consistency with N=8–16 is the default production choice for maths-heavy workloads. At N=16 you recover ≈70% of the gain that a trained PRM selector would give, at zero extra model-training cost. Budget is the primary signal: add a PRM only when N>32 is insufficient and you have >50k labelled examples to train on.

09

What to Take Away

  • ORMs are outcome-level; PRMs are step-level. PRMs give denser, more accurate reward signal; ORMs are cheaper to train. For most production use, start with an ORM and promote to a PRM when you have >50k step-labelled examples.
  • PRM800K and Math-Shepherd set the benchmark. Human labels (PRM800K, 800k steps) beat MC-automated labels (Math-Shepherd, 390k steps) by ≈3 pp but at ≈100× the cost. MC estimation is the practical path to scale.
  • MCTS + PRM is the Pareto frontier for hard problems. At equal compute, MCTS with a PRM outperforms best-of-N with an ORM when base-model accuracy is below ∼50% greedy. The UCB selection drives exploration to under-visited promising branches.
  • Self-consistency is the zero-cost baseline. Best-of-N majority vote with N=8–16 requires no verifier and recovers most of the ensemble gain. It fails when the model has systematic biases.
  • ToT without an external scorer is unreliable. LLM self-evaluation of intermediate steps is poorly calibrated on formal maths. Use a trained PRM or symbolic checker, not prompting alone.
  • Cost-quality curves cross over by difficulty. Invest in MCTS/PRM only for hard tasks; for easy tasks, greedy + self-consistency is cheapest.
